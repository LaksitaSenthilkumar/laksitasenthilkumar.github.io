How I work

Three habits behind every project

01

Start with the decision

At MGM Paper Boxes, management needed clearer visibility. I built the dashboards around the decisions they had to make, not around the spreadsheet I had.

02

Look for the driver, not the total

In customer spending, income and region explained more than anything else I tested. In fraud, the 6% minority class was the whole problem, so I designed around it.

03

Check the model before anyone trusts it

My fraud model's train-test F1 gap is 0.002. That small gap is how I know it learned the pattern rather than memorizing the training claims.

Making private markets legible

Machine learning · Insurance

Catching fraud in a 6% haystack

Fraud is rare in this data: 6% of 15,400+ auto claims. A model can look accurate while missing most of it. Mine is built to find the fraud that matters.

PythonSMOTELogistic RegressionDecision Tree
Read the case study
  • The problem: With only 6% fraud, accuracy alone rewards a model that misses the fraud.
  • The data: 15,400+ auto insurance claims.
  • The method: Outlier capping, encoding, engineered features (96 reduced to 65 by correlation and variance filtering), SMOTE and class weighting, stratified splits.
  • Result: 97% fraud recall, with a train-test F1 gap of 0.002.
  • Next: Set the decision threshold from investigator capacity, not from accuracy alone.

ExcelVBAPivot tablesSlicers

About

Why data, and why now

I grew up in Tamil Nadu, India, in a family that ran gold jewelry and agricultural export businesses. Those businesses taught me that pricing, supply and trust are really one conversation, and that the number on the invoice always has a story behind it.

The clearest test of that idea came during seven days at a government school in my district, where I helped build a toilet facility for students and taught basic lessons to local children. It made me ask a question I still use: will this actually change something for the people it is meant to help?

That question is why I study business analytics at UMass Amherst's Isenberg School. I want to work where data changes what someone does on Monday morning, and where I can grow toward product management.
